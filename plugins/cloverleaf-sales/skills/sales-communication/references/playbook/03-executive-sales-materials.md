# World-class executive sales materials

**Purpose:** A research-backed standard for executive one-pagers, sales proposals, business cases, and PowerPoint presentations used during B2B and B2G evaluations.

**Research updated:** August 11, 2026

**Example safety:** Any names, dates, organizations, or metrics used in sample headlines, patterns, or worked examples are fictional illustrations and must be replaced. Cited research findings, accessibility thresholds, and internal rubric weights are not part of that disclaimer.

## The standard in one sentence

An excellent executive asset is a trustworthy decision aid: it states the recommendation first, connects the buying group's shared objectives, compares credible alternatives, quantifies value with traceable assumptions, makes risk visible, and ends with a precise action.

It is not a brochure with better formatting.

## The executive test

An executive should be able to scan the first page or opening slides and answer:

1. What decision is requested?
2. What is recommended?
3. Why act now?
4. What outcome changes?
5. What does it cost?
6. Why should we believe it?
7. What could prevent the result?
8. Who owns delivery and measurement?
9. What happens next?

If the asset makes the reader discover the point, it is not executive-ready.

## What the evidence supports

### Buying-group consensus is part of the job

Gartner's 2025 survey of 632 B2B buyers found that 74% of buying teams experienced unhealthy conflict and that groups reaching consensus were 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal. Gartner also warns that content narrowly tailored to individual roles can undermine the shared understanding a group needs. See [Gartner's buying-group consensus research](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process) and [B2B buying journey guidance](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey).

The operating rule is:

> Address stakeholder-specific questions inside one coherent, shared business case.

Do not create five incompatible stories for five people.

### Buyers need enablement, not more promotion

Forrester describes B2B buying groups repeatedly building a case to investigate, compare, recommend, approve, and measure a purchase. See [Why Marketing Should Build Better Buyer Enablement Content](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/why-marketing-should-build-better-buyer-enablement-content/).

Great material helps the customer complete those jobs:

- Define the problem
- Agree on outcomes
- Establish requirements
- Compare alternatives
- Validate a recommendation
- Build internal consensus
- Plan implementation
- Define success

### Decision rigor creates credibility

HM Treasury's 2026 Green Book treats a business case as an evidence-based comparison of objectives, options, costs, benefits, risks, uncertainty, affordability, commercial feasibility, and delivery. It explicitly warns against reducing a decision to one ratio. See [The Green Book 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book-appraisal-and-evaluation-in-central-government/the-green-book-2026).

The standards were written for public investment, but the rigor transfers well to enterprise sales. Apply it proportionately.

### Clear design reduces cognitive burden

Richard Mayer's multimedia research supports removing extraneous material, signaling what matters, and placing explanatory words near the visual they explain. See the [Cambridge Handbook summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44).

Assertion-evidence research also found stronger comprehension and recall when slides used sentence headlines stating the main claim and visual evidence supporting it, rather than topic titles and bullet lists. See [Garner and Alley's assertion-evidence research](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/how-the-design-of-presentation-slides-affects-audience-comprehens/).

These studies are not sales-conversion experiments. They are strong evidence for comprehension design, which is necessary but not sufficient for persuasion.

## Shared characteristics of excellence

### 1. Decision first

State the recommendation and requested action before background. Use a headline that answers, not a label that introduces a topic.

| Better | Worse |
|---|---|
| Approve a 90-day pilot with expansion tied to measured pipeline lift | Pilot overview |
| The preferred option pays back in 8 to 11 months | ROI analysis |
| A narrower launch protects the November deadline | Implementation |

### 2. One primary job per asset

A one-pager can secure permission for a pilot. A proposal can define the offer. A business case can justify the investment. A presentation can drive a decision in the room.

Do not ask one asset to educate the category, introduce the company, explain every feature, compare competitors, prove ROI, resolve security, negotiate price, and close the deal.

### 3. Buyer language and account evidence

Use:

- The organization's stated objectives
- The buyer's terminology
- Verified quotes, signals, and decision criteria
- The buyer's baseline and constraints
- The buyer's timeline and approval path

Avoid:

- Generic persona pain presented as account fact
- Seller category language before the buyer's problem
- Internal CRM stages
- Boilerplate that could be sent to any account

### 4. One coherent argument

A strong asset connects:

> **Pain → business impact → desired outcome → options → recommendation → value → proof → risk → delivery → decision**

Every section should support this argument. Remove content that does not.

### 5. Value, proof, risk, and delivery stay together

- **Value:** What measurable or strategically important change occurs?
- **Proof:** Why should the buyer believe it?
- **Risk:** What could prevent the result?
- **Delivery:** How will the result actually be produced and measured?

A feature without a buyer outcome is description. A benefit without proof is a claim. A benefit without a delivery plan is aspiration. A polished page without source discipline is decoration.

### 6. Credible alternatives

Compare the recommendation with real choices, including business as usual. Use the same scope, time horizon, assumptions, and risk treatment for each option.

Do not compare the seller's preferred solution with an exaggerated do-nothing scenario.

### 7. Honest uncertainty

Separate:

- Facts from estimates
- Customer data from external benchmarks
- Observed results from modeled outcomes
- Confirmed scope from assumptions
- Gross potential from attributable benefit
- Base case from upside

Use ranges when precision is not justified. Label what must be validated.

### 8. Forwardability

Assume the champion will forward the asset to a CFO, operator, security reviewer, procurement lead, and executive sponsor without the seller present.

The asset should:

- Explain the decision without narration
- Use consistent terms, numbers, and scope
- Anticipate predictable questions
- Preserve essential caveats
- Make sources and dates easy to find
- Give the champion language they can repeat accurately

## Stakeholder lens

| Stakeholder | Primary questions the shared case must answer |
|---|---|
| CEO or senior executive | Does this advance a strategic objective? Why now? What decision is required? |
| CFO or finance | Is it affordable? What is the cash impact, return, payback, uncertainty, and downside? |
| Operating leader | What process changes? Who adopts it? What capacity or outcome improves? |
| IT and security | How does it integrate? What data, controls, architecture, and operational dependencies apply? |
| Procurement and legal | Is scope clear? Are terms, obligations, pricing, risk allocation, and exit conditions workable? |
| Champion | Can I explain this simply, defend it, and move the group to the next decision? |

Role-specific callouts are useful. Separate role-specific narratives that contradict the shared objective are not.

## Choose the right format

| Format | Best use | Core question | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive one-pager | Secure one decision, align leaders, or create a forwardable summary | What should we do and why? | Miniature brochure |
| Proposal | Define a compliant, responsive, differentiated offer | What exactly are we offering, why us, at what terms? | Seller biography plus boilerplate |
| Business case | Justify change and investment across stakeholders | Is this the best use of resources versus alternatives? | Unsupported ROI advocacy |
| Live PowerPoint | Drive understanding, discussion, and decision in a room | What must the audience believe and decide? | Dense leave-behind projected on screen |
| Read-ahead or leave-behind deck | Travel without a presenter | Can a non-attendee reconstruct the full case? | Cryptic live slides with no context |

## Shared accessibility standard

Accessibility applies to one-pagers, proposals, business cases, slides, and exported PDFs, not only to live presentations.

- Use semantic headings and a logical heading order.
- Preserve selectable text; do not turn paragraphs or tables into images.
- Use a tagged PDF when exporting and verify the tag tree.
- Set a logical reading order independent of visual placement.
- Add meaningful alt text to informative visuals and mark decorative objects appropriately.
- Give links descriptive text instead of “click here.”
- Identify table headers and avoid unnecessarily complex tables.
- Set the document language and meaningful title metadata.
- Do not use color alone to convey status or meaning.
- Check text and meaningful graphic contrast in the delivered format.

Use current format-specific accessibility checkers, then inspect the exported artifact manually. The U.S. Section 508 PDF testing guidance covers tags, selectable content, reading order, document language, and alt text. WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. See [Section 508 PDF testing](https://www.section508.gov/training/pdfs/aed-cop-pdf02/) and [W3C's WCAG 2.2 contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

# Executive one-pagers

## Purpose

A one-pager should enable one specific decision or next step. It is not a compressed data sheet and not a place to shrink a long proposal until it technically fits.

### Appropriate jobs

- Approve a pilot or evaluation phase
- Align an executive sponsor on the problem and recommendation
- Equip a champion for an internal meeting
- Summarize a proposal or business case
- Present a signal-led account opportunity
- Resolve one material objection or decision tension

## Recommended content

1. **Decision headline**
   - State the recommendation or finding.
2. **Decision requested**
   - Name the exact approval, commitment, choice, or meeting.
3. **Why now**
   - Use a verified trigger, deadline, priority, pain, or cost of delay.
4. **Current-state evidence**
   - Use two or three account-specific facts, signals, or quotes.
5. **Recommended course**
   - Explain what changes, for whom, and why this approach fits.
6. **Value at stake**
   - Use one or two material metrics with baseline, time horizon, and assumptions.
7. **Proof**
   - Include a relevant result, benchmark, source, or comparable case.
8. **Risk and mitigation**
   - Address the one or two concerns most likely to block the decision.
9. **Owner, next step, and version**
   - State owner, timing, source dates, and “as of” date.

## One-page blueprint

| Zone | Content | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Top band | Recommendation, decision requested, and why now | Can the reader understand the ask in 10 seconds? |
| Left body | Current-state facts and business impact | Are facts account-specific and sourced? |
| Center body | Recommended approach and how it changes the outcome | Is the causal logic clear? |
| Right body | Value, proof, and top risk | Is the claim credible and bounded? |
| Bottom band | Owner, date, next action, sources, and version | Can the champion forward it unchanged? |

The exact grid is a design choice, not a universal law. Preserve one reading path and visible hierarchy.

## Layout standards

- Put the recommendation and key number above supporting detail.
- Use descriptive headings that make sense without the body copy.
- Keep one main visual only when it reduces decision effort.
- Give evidence, value, risk, and the ask distinct visual zones.
- Preserve comfortable type and white space.
- Move methodology and deep technical detail to a linked appendix.
- Do not hide decisive caveats in tiny footnotes.
- Use a visible source and version line.

Digital.gov's plain-language guidance supports audience-first organization, concise sections, descriptive headings, short paragraphs, and front-loaded summaries. See [Principles of plain language](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles), [Headings](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/headings), and [Clear and short](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short).

## Fictional excellent pattern

> **Approve a 90-day pilot to reduce bid-research time by an estimated 30% to 40%, with expansion contingent on measured adoption and accuracy.**

It states the action, time period, outcome, range, and risk control.

## Weak patterns

- “Company X + Vendor Y: Transforming the Future”
- Five paragraphs of seller history
- A logo wall without context or results
- Three unrelated CTAs
- A large ROI number with no baseline or assumptions
- “Low risk” without a named risk, mitigation, and owner
- Tiny type used to make a two-page argument fit on one page

# Sales proposals

## The standard

A world-class proposal is compliant, responsive, differentiated, evidenced, commercially clear, and easy to evaluate.

### Compliance comes first

For a formal solicitation, the buyer's instructions override every generic template.

FAR 15.305 requires agencies to evaluate proposals solely against the factors and subfactors stated in the solicitation. Where applicable, it addresses cost or price, past performance, technical ability, and documented strengths, weaknesses, deficiencies, and risks. See [FAR 15.305](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/15.305).

Therefore:

- Mirror required organization and numbering.
- Answer every requirement explicitly.
- Maintain a compliance matrix.
- Allocate effort and space in proportion to evaluation weight.
- Make evidence easy to locate.
- Distinguish mandatory compliance from differentiating value.
- Check every volume, appendix, and pricing schedule for consistency.

## The proposal argument

Each major section should answer:

1. What does the buyer need or require?
2. What do we propose?
3. How does it work?
4. What outcome does it produce for the buyer?
5. Why is this approach meaningfully different?
6. What proof supports the claim?
7. What delivery risk remains, and how is it controlled?

A useful proposal-theme formula is:

> **Buyer priority → measurable benefit → relevant discriminator → proof → risk control**

Shipley's proposal guidance emphasizes customer focus, requirement-to-benefit logic, discriminators, evidence, risk mitigation, and informative visual titles. See the [Proposal Writer Playbook](https://digital.shipleywins.com/view/320547). APMP's study guides cover executive summaries and bid-and-proposal writing; downloading them may require checkout or sign-in. See the official [Executive Summaries](https://www.apmp.org/Web/Web/Contact_Management/Item_Detail.aspx?Category=PUB&iProductCode=ESMC2024) and [Bid & Proposal Writing](https://members.apmp.org/ItemDetail?Category=PUB&iProductCode=BPWMC2024) resource pages.

## Executive summary

Write it early enough to guide the proposal strategy, then revise it after the body is complete.

It should:

- Describe the buyer's desired future state.
- Show understanding of the current problem and consequence.
- State the recommended approach.
- Present the two to four most important win themes.
- Quantify buyer value where supportable.
- Provide relevant proof.
- Summarize implementation, risk, and ownership.
- State the investment or commercial headline when appropriate and permitted.
- End with the procedural or commercial next step.

It should not be:

- A table of contents
- A company biography
- A generic capability summary
- A collage of section introductions
- A place to introduce unsupported claims that the proposal never proves

APMP suggests executive-summary length conventions, but no percentage applies universally. The right length is the shortest version that lets the evaluator understand and defend the recommendation.

## Recommended structure when the buyer has not prescribed one

1. Decision-led executive summary
2. Customer situation, objectives, and success criteria
3. Recommended solution and scope
4. Outcome and value case
5. Delivery plan, milestones, governance, and adoption
6. Relevant proof, team, and past performance
7. Risk, security, compliance, and dependencies
8. Pricing, terms, assumptions, and exclusions
9. Decision process and next steps
10. Technical detail and compliance matrix in appendices

## Commercial clarity

Make these visible:

- Included and excluded scope
- One-time and recurring costs
- Customer resource requirements
- Pricing basis and validity period
- Dependencies
- Implementation responsibilities
- Optional items
- Renewal and escalation assumptions
- Acceptance or success criteria
- Data, integration, security, and legal conditions
- Exit, transition, or termination responsibilities when material

Ambiguity increases perceived risk. Do not hide a hard truth in a footnote and expect trust to survive discovery.

## Proposal review gates

Run distinct reviews rather than one general proofread:

1. **Compliance review:** Does every requirement have a complete, traceable answer?
2. **Customer review:** Does the proposal use the buyer's priorities and language?
3. **Strategy review:** Are differentiators relevant, provable, and repeated consistently?
4. **Evidence review:** Does every material claim have proof?
5. **Commercial review:** Do scope, price, assumptions, terms, and exclusions align?
6. **Risk and delivery review:** Is the plan feasible, owned, and measured?
7. **Executive review:** Can a senior reader understand the recommendation and decision quickly?
8. **Production review:** Are numbers, names, dates, links, cross-references, accessibility, and file requirements correct?

# Business cases

## The standard

A world-class business case shows that the recommendation is the best available use of resources under explicit assumptions. It is not a vendor ROI calculator with the inconvenient costs removed.

## Use the Five Case Model as a completeness audit

HM Treasury's Five Case Model provides five connected dimensions:

| Case | Question for a commercial evaluation |
|---|---|
| Strategic | Why change? What objective, current-state problem, and strategic fit justify action? |
| Economic | What credible options exist, and which creates the best overall value? |
| Commercial | Can the parties form a workable deal with clear scope and risk allocation? |
| Financial | Is the preferred option affordable, and what is the cash impact? |
| Management | Can the organization implement, govern, adopt, measure, and sustain it? |

These labels do not need to appear in the final asset. The questions do.

## Compare real options

At minimum consider:

- Business as usual
- Do minimum
- Recommended option
- A credible alternative where one exists

Apply the same:

- Scope
- Time horizon
- Baseline
- Discounting
- Benefit definitions
- Cost categories
- Risk treatment

State why each option was included or rejected.

## Economic measures

Use only the measures that fit the decision. Define them.

### Total cost of ownership

Include material lifecycle costs:

- Acquisition and subscription
- Implementation and integration
- Internal labor
- Data preparation
- Training and change management
- Ongoing administration and support
- Security, compliance, and vendor management
- Growth or usage costs
- Exit and transition costs
- Contingency where appropriate

### ROI

```text
ROI (%) = [(total monetized benefits - total costs) / total costs] × 100%
```

State whether costs and benefits are nominal or discounted, the time horizon, and what was excluded. If presenting ROI as a ratio instead of a percentage, label it clearly.

### Net present value

```text
NPV = Σ from t=0 to T of [(B_t - C_t) / (1 + r)^t]
```

Where `B_t` is benefit in period `t`, `C_t` is cost in period `t`, `r` is the discount rate, and `T` is the analysis horizon. Use the buyer's approved discount rate. Do not invent one because a benchmark used it. Match real cash flows with a real discount rate, or nominal cash flows with a nominal rate; never mix them.

### Benefit-cost ratio

```text
BCR = present value of benefits / present value of costs
```

Do not treat this ratio as the whole decision. HM Treasury explicitly requires decision-makers to consider objectives, unmonetized impacts, affordability, risk, uncertainty, and distribution alongside summary metrics.

### Payback

The first period in which cumulative net cash flow becomes non-negative. State whether cash flows are discounted and show the period granularity.

### Cost of delay

Model only supportable components:

```text
Cost of delay = attributable current-state cost + attributable foregone benefit + risk exposure over the delay period
```

Do not automatically add all three terms. Current-state cost and foregone benefit can overlap; identify and remove double counting. Quantify risk exposure as probability-weighted expected loss only when both probability and impact are supportable. Otherwise describe the risk qualitatively. Do not use fear-based cost-of-inaction claims without a source, owner, and calculation.

Never sum benefits that describe the same underlying value. For example, labor hours released, labor-cost avoidance, and headcount savings may be three labels for one benefit rather than three additive benefits.

Forrester's Total Economic Impact framework is a useful commercial cross-check because it considers costs, benefits, flexibility, and risk. See [Forrester's TEI methodology](https://www.forrester.com/policies/tei/). Commissioned TEI studies remain commissioned research and should not be presented as guaranteed customer outcomes.

## Benefit logic

Connect each benefit:

> **Product output → operational outcome → measurable benefit → strategic objective**

Example:

> Territory-specific signal delivered daily → less manual meeting research → more qualified pre-RFP opportunities per rep → stronger pipeline coverage and forecast confidence

Every arrow needs evidence or an explicit assumption.

## Benefit taxonomy

Classify benefits before monetizing them:

| Benefit type | Meaning | Evidence standard |
|---|---|---|
| Cash savings | A budgeted cash outflow actually decreases | Show the affected line item, timing, and accountable owner |
| Cost avoidance | A supportable future cost does not occur | Show the counterfactual, trigger, timing, and probability |
| Released capacity or productivity | The same resources can do more or redirect time | Show hours, adoption, utilization, and the work that will absorb capacity |
| Revenue contribution | The initiative influences incremental or protected revenue | Show the funnel logic, attribution, timing, and competing causes |
| Risk reduction | Expected frequency or impact of an adverse event declines | Show the exposure, control effect, probability basis, and residual risk |

Hours saved are not cash savings unless budget, headcount, overtime, contractor spend, or another cash outflow actually changes. Keep modeled productivity separate from cashable value.

## Assumption ledger

For each material benefit or cost, capture:

| Field | Required detail |
|---|---|
| Baseline | Current measured performance or cost |
| Unit | Hours, dollars, opportunities, incidents, days, or another defined measure |
| Expected change | Range and direction |
| Population or volume | People, transactions, accounts, or events affected |
| Adoption ramp | When and how usage reaches the modeled level |
| Time to value | Delay before benefit begins |
| Attribution | Portion reasonably attributable to the initiative |
| Source | Customer actual, official data, independent benchmark, peer result, vendor benchmark, or assumption |
| Source date | Freshness and period represented |
| Confidence | High, medium, or low with rationale |
| Owner | Person accountable for validation |
| Measurement plan | System, frequency, and review cadence |

Never mix customer actuals, external benchmarks, and working assumptions without labels.

## Scenario and sensitivity analysis

Make uncertainty analysis proportionate to the size, irreversibility, and risk of the decision. Material investment cases should show:

- Downside case
- Base case
- Upside case
- Variables driving the result
- A switching value where practical, meaning the point at which the recommendation no longer creates value or no longer beats another option

Stress-test the most uncertain inputs:

- Adoption
- Time saved
- Conversion or win impact
- Implementation duration
- Internal labor
- Integration cost
- Benefit ramp
- Renewal or growth cost

The Green Book, OMB Circular A-94, and the GAO Cost Estimating Guide all emphasize uncertainty and sensitivity. See [OMB Circular A-94](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/CircularA-94.pdf) and the [GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide](https://www.gao.gov/pdf/product/705312).

## Correct for optimism bias

Business cases tend to underestimate cost and duration and overestimate benefit. Counter this by:

- Using ranges rather than false precision
- Comparing assumptions with actual results from similar deployments
- Including adoption and ramp time
- Reviewing historical forecast error
- Increasing cost or reducing benefit in the downside case
- Assigning an owner to validate each material assumption
- Showing what would disprove the case

## Benefits realization

Approval is not realization. For each major benefit, define:

- Baseline
- Target
- Date
- Metric source
- Owner
- Review cadence
- Corrective action if performance misses the target

Revisit the business case during implementation. A business case that disappears after signature was sales collateral, not a management instrument.

# Executive PowerPoint presentations

## First decide whether the deck is live or standalone

Duarte distinguishes projected slides from self-contained read-aheads or “slidedocs.” Live slides support a speaker. Read-aheads must carry the reasoning without one. See [The slides you deliver versus the Slidedoc you leave behind](https://www.duarte.com/blog/the-slides-you-deliver-versus-the-slidedoc-you-leave-behind/).

### Live deck

- Sparse and visual
- One main claim per slide
- Speaker supplies nuance and transitions
- Detail moves to notes or appendix
- Built for the room, screen, and available time

### Read-ahead or leave-behind

- Self-contained
- More explicit evidence, context, and source notes
- Clear navigation
- Enough prose to preserve reasoning
- Designed for scanning and later forwarding

Trying to make one file do both usually creates a live deck that is too dense and a leave-behind that is too cryptic. Build two versions when the decision warrants it.

## Reliable executive storyline

There is no universal slide count. A useful sequence is:

1. **Decision and recommendation**
2. **Why action is needed now**
3. **Evidence defining the problem or opportunity**
4. **Options considered**
5. **Recommended approach and why it wins**
6. **Economics and measurable outcomes**
7. **Delivery plan, governance, and proof**
8. **Risks, trade-offs, and mitigations**
9. **Decision requested and next step**

Compress or expand based on complexity and meeting time. Do not use a fixed slide rule as a substitute for judgment.

## Slide-level standard

Each slide should have:

1. One sentence headline that states the claim or takeaway
2. One primary visual or evidence structure supporting that claim
3. Direct labels and concise annotations
4. Source, period, unit, and material caveat for quantitative evidence
5. Only the detail needed to understand the point

### Assertion headline examples

| Better | Worse |
|---|---|
| Manual research consumes 18 hours per opportunity | Current process |
| The pilot isolates value before broader rollout | Pilot approach |
| Adoption is the largest driver of payback | Sensitivity analysis |

The body must prove the headline. If it does not, either the evidence or the claim is wrong.

## Title-only test

Read only the slide titles in order. They should tell a coherent argument from problem to decision.

If the titles read “Overview, Challenges, Platform, Features, Integrations, Customers, Pricing, Next Steps,” the deck has topics, not a storyline.

## Data visualization

The UK's Office for National Statistics recommends chart titles that communicate the main trend, concise active wording, clear labels, limited clutter, and visible sources. See [ONS chart text guidance](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/chart-text).

Use these standards:

- Choose the simplest encoding that answers the decision question.
- Highlight one comparison and neutralize the rest.
- Label data directly when practical.
- Put annotations beside the data they explain.
- Show absolute values alongside percentages when the base matters.
- Use consistent units, periods, and baselines.
- Do not truncate an axis in a way that exaggerates the story.
- Avoid unnecessary 3D, shadows, heavy borders, and decorative dashboards.
- Do not use color as the sole carrier of meaning.

## Accessibility

Microsoft recommends unique descriptive slide titles, 18-point or larger text, readable sans-serif type, whitespace, sufficient contrast, meaningful alt text, logical reading order, and alternatives to color-only meaning. See [Make PowerPoint presentations accessible](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/accessibility/powerpoint/make-your-powerpoint-presentations-accessible-to-people-with-disabilities).

Use 18 point as a minimum, not a design target. A live executive deck will often need larger type because of room size and display conditions.

For contrast, WCAG's normal-text minimum is 4.5:1 and its large-text minimum is 3:1. See [W3C WCAG 2.2 contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

Run PowerPoint's accessibility checker, then inspect contrast, reading order, alt text, tables, links, and mobile or PDF rendering manually.

## Presentation QA

- **Headline-only test:** Do titles tell the complete argument?
- **Evidence test:** Does each slide prove its headline?
- **Glance test:** Can the live audience identify the point quickly?
- **Narration test:** Does the speaker add insight instead of reading the slide?
- **Forwarding test:** Can a non-attendee understand the recommendation?
- **Question test:** Are finance, security, risk, procurement, and implementation concerns covered?
- **Accessibility test:** Does the file work for different visual and assistive needs?
- **Time test:** Is there room for discussion and interruption?

# Decision psychology and ethical persuasion

## Executives scan before they read

Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking research shows that digital readers often scan, with early content, headings, bullets, and visual grouping receiving disproportionate attention. Its F-shaped pattern is especially associated with weakly formatted text and is a failure pattern, not a layout target. Do not merely stack important content down the left edge. Applying web evidence to PDFs is an inference, but the case for descriptive headings and meaningful visual grouping is sensible. See [F-shaped reading pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/).

Therefore:

- Put the answer and material number early.
- Front-load headings with information-bearing words.
- Use visual groups and short sections.
- Treat the first page or screen as decisive.

## Clear design can create false confidence

Research on processing fluency found that easy-to-read statements can feel more true. See [Reber and Schwarz on perceptual fluency](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053810099903860).

This creates an ethical requirement: polish must never substitute for evidence. Visible sources, assumptions, and uncertainty protect the decision.

## Framing affects choice

Tversky and Kahneman showed that equivalent choices can lead to different preferences under different gain and loss frames. See [The Framing of Decisions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7455683/).

Responsible practice:

- Show gains and avoided losses where relevant.
- Include business as usual as a real comparator.
- Use equal time horizons and assumptions.
- Show absolute values with percentages.
- Make excluded costs and benefits explicit.
- Avoid fear-based cost-of-inaction claims.

# Proof and risk standards

## Default evidence preference

Start with the most decision-relevant evidence available:

1. Customer's measured baseline or actual result
2. Audited, regulatory, or official data
3. Relevant peer result with comparable scale and use case
4. Independent third-party benchmark
5. Vendor benchmark with transparent methodology
6. Approved qualitative testimonial
7. Unsupported assertion

This ordering is not absolute. A customer actual may be highly relevant but poorly measured; audited or independent evidence may be methodologically stronger but less comparable. Evaluate each item on:

- Relevance to the decision and account
- Independence and disclosed conflicts
- Methodology and sample quality
- Freshness
- Comparability of audience, scale, use case, and conditions

Choose the strongest overall fit, not merely the highest source label. An unsupported assertion is not proof, even when it appears in a large font.

For each important proof point, state where applicable:

- Customer or context
- Baseline
- Result
- Time period
- Sample or volume
- Source and date
- Attribution limits
- Permission status for names, quotes, logos, and results

## Risk treatment

At minimum consider:

- Adoption and change
- Implementation capacity
- Integration and data quality
- Privacy, security, and compliance
- Cost and schedule
- Procurement and legal dependencies
- Supplier continuity
- Benefits failing to materialize

For each material risk, show:

- Likelihood
- Impact
- Trigger
- Mitigation
- Owner
- Residual risk
- Contingency

“Low risk” is not a risk assessment.

# AI creation contract

Always require these inputs before drafting:

```text
Asset type: one-pager, proposal, business case, live deck, read-ahead
Audience: decision maker, buying group, evaluators, and hidden stakeholders
Decision requested: exact approval, choice, or next action
Buyer objective: verified and sourceable
Why now: trigger, deadline, consequence, and source
Verified source list and as-of date
Brand, accessibility, confidentiality, and submission constraints
```

Require these when material to the asset's job and decision stage:

```text
Current state: baseline, process, cost, risk, and owner
Options: business as usual, do minimum, recommendation, alternatives
Recommendation: what changes and why it is preferred
Economics: costs, benefits, horizon, discount rate, attribution, ramp
Proof: approved customer evidence, public data, or benchmarks
Risks: likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner, residual risk
Delivery: scope, milestones, responsibilities, dependencies, governance
Success measures: baseline, target, date, source, owner
Commercial facts: price, terms, inclusions, exclusions, validity
```

Record which conditional inputs are not material and why. If a critical input is missing, do not disguise it with polished prose. Mark it as an open assumption, ask for it, or narrow the recommendation.

# AI evaluation method

## Scoring anchor

Score each category from 0 to 4:

- **4:** Specific, decision-ready, quantified where appropriate, and traceable
- **3:** Complete and credible with minor weaknesses
- **2:** Partly complete but generic, unclear, or lightly supported
- **1:** Mostly absent or materially weak
- **0:** Missing, contradicted, fabricated, or noncompliant

Weighted score:

```text
category weight × category score / 4
```

The 80- and 90-point thresholds below are internal quality-control policy, not research findings or predicted win rates.

For every scored category, the AI must return:

```text
Category and score
Cited evidence from the asset
Defect or gap
Recommended fix
Confidence: high, medium, or low with rationale
```

Use `N/A` only when buyer instructions or the asset's legitimate job make a category inapplicable, such as a compliant early-stage brief that cannot include pricing. Do not award automatic full credit. Document the reason and normalize the score across applicable weights:

```text
normalized score = weighted points earned / total applicable weight × 100
```

## Critical gates

Do not approve an asset containing:

- A fabricated quote, customer result, source, name, date, or citation
- Materially inconsistent arithmetic
- A violation of explicit proposal instructions
- A material metric without a source or assumption trail
- Hidden material scope, cost, dependency, or exclusion
- Confidential information used without authorization
- A customer name, logo, result, or quote without permission or an approved public source

Cap below 80 if the asset lacks an explicit recommendation or ask, risk treatment when material, or enough context to work without undocumented narration.

## Automated red-team checks

Before approving an AI-created asset:

1. Extract every number and recompute the arithmetic.
2. Extract every factual claim and map it to a source.
3. Open every cited link, confirm that it resolves, and verify that the source actually entails the adjacent claim.
4. Label every number as actual, benchmark, estimate, or assumption.
5. Read only the headings and summarize the argument.
6. Evaluate through CFO, operator, security, procurement, and executive-sponsor lenses.
7. Name the strongest objection the asset does not answer.
8. Compare scope, pricing, claims, dates, and terminology across related emails, proposals, and decks.
9. Test whether the champion can forward the asset unchanged.
10. Run the required accessibility checks.
11. Verify the latest brand, product, legal, and partner-brand inputs.

# Final quality checklists

## Universal

- [ ] The decision and recommendation appear first.
- [ ] The asset has one primary job and one primary ask.
- [ ] The buyer's objectives and language lead the story.
- [ ] The argument connects pain, impact, options, recommendation, value, proof, risk, delivery, and decision.
- [ ] Business as usual and credible alternatives are treated fairly.
- [ ] Every material fact and number is sourced and current.
- [ ] Actuals, benchmarks, estimates, and assumptions are labeled.
- [ ] Costs, scope, dependencies, and exclusions are visible.
- [ ] The material risks have owners and mitigations.
- [ ] The implementation and measurement plan is credible.
- [ ] The asset works when forwarded without the seller.
- [ ] The same terms and numbers appear across all related assets.
- [ ] The asset clears 90 points and no critical gate fails.

## One-pager

- [ ] The recommendation, ask, why-now, and key number are visible in 10 seconds.
- [ ] The page uses one clear reading path.
- [ ] Account evidence, value, proof, risk, and next step have visible hierarchy.
- [ ] Essential caveats are legible.
- [ ] The source and version line is present.

## Proposal

- [ ] Every instruction and evaluation criterion is mapped and answered.
- [ ] The executive summary is buyer-focused and decision-led.
- [ ] Differentiators are relevant, provable, and tied to buyer benefit.
- [ ] Commercial scope, terms, price, assumptions, and exclusions align.
- [ ] Past performance is relevant, current, contextualized, and approved.
- [ ] Cross-references, appendices, file format, and submission requirements are correct.

## Business case

- [ ] The case for change and strategic fit are explicit.
- [ ] Options use a consistent baseline, horizon, and scope.
- [ ] All material lifecycle costs are included.
- [ ] Benefit attribution and adoption ramp are realistic.
- [ ] Proportionate uncertainty analysis is present; material investment cases include downside, base, and upside scenarios.
- [ ] Sensitivity and switching variables are visible.
- [ ] Each major benefit has a baseline, target, date, source, and owner.

## PowerPoint

- [ ] The deck is intentionally live or standalone.
- [ ] Slide titles tell the argument without body text.
- [ ] Each slide contains one main claim and supporting evidence.
- [ ] Quantitative slides show source, period, unit, and caveat.
- [ ] Charts are simple, honest, and directly labeled.
- [ ] Type, contrast, reading order, alt text, tables, and color use are accessible.
- [ ] The deck leaves room for discussion.

# Claims not to encode as universal rules

- A fixed slide count
- A fixed word count for every one-pager or executive summary
- A fixed number of bullets, proof points, or sections
- A guaranteed ROI threshold
- A specific layout grid as the only correct design
- “One idea per slide” as permission to split one weak idea across many slides
- A beautiful design as evidence that a claim is true
- Persona-level tailoring as a replacement for a shared buying-group case
- A customer benchmark as a guaranteed outcome for another customer
