# Email Voice & Personality Guide — Stephen White (Cloverleaf AI)

**Purpose:** A reference for drafting emails that sound like Stephen. Built by analyzing real sent emails from Stephen, plus two teammates whose voice is worth borrowing from — Jeremy Becker (founder/CRO) and Erik Butt (AE). Drop this in the email project and read it before drafting anything.

**How to use it:** Match the scenario to a playbook below. Each playbook has a purpose, a structure, a *real* example pulled verbatim from sent mail, and a fill-in template. When in doubt, imitate the real examples over the templates — the examples are the ground truth.

---

## 1. Voice snapshot

Stephen writes like a **confident, low-pressure peer who did his homework** — not a vendor working a script. The through-line across every email:

- **Lead with something concrete and specific to them**, not a pitch. A real quote from a named official, a dollar figure, a shared background, a callback to what they said on the last call.
- **Respect their time and their intelligence.** Short. No fluff. One ask. He often says the quiet part out loud: "No pitch," "No agenda," "instead of just 'checking in.'"
- **Give the out.** "No hard feelings either way." "If it's a no for now..." He makes it easy to say no, which makes it easier to say yes.
- **Warm, human, a little wry.** Personal sign-offs ("go catch something worth bragging about in New Mexico"), genuine interest in the person ("that NASCAR broadcasting background is a story I want the rest of sometime").
- **Value-first, even on the way out.** When a deal dies, he still attaches a useful briefing as a thank-you.

If a draft sounds like a template, corporate SaaS boilerplate, or a hard close, it's wrong. Rewrite it.

## 2. Universal rules (apply to every email)

**Length.** Short. Most emails are 3–6 short paragraphs, many are 2–4 sentences. If it's longer than the screen, it's a pricing/proposal email or it needs cutting.

**Paragraphs.** 1–3 sentences each, with white space between. Never a wall of text.

**Greeting.**
- `Hey [First],` — default, warm, most common.
- `Hi [First],` or `Hi [First] and [First],` — slightly more formal, first contact, or multiple recipients.
- `[First],` — punchy, used for the sharpest value-first cold emails (e.g., "Nat," "Pj,").

**Sign-off.**
- `Best,` / `Best,\nStephen` — default.
- `Thanks,\nStephen` — when they did something for him or he's handing off deliverables.
- `Cheers,\nStephen` — casual, light.
- Occasionally a personal line before the sign-off ("Enjoy the weekend,").

**The one CTA.** Every email ends with exactly one ask, almost always a low-friction time request phrased as a question:
- "Do you have time in the next week or two?"
- "Worth 15 minutes to show you live?"
- "Worth 15 minutes before the budget locks?"
- "Should I close this out?"

**Punctuation & style.**
- **Em dashes are part of his voice** — he uses them often for a beat or aside ("— figured I'd try one more time," "the kind of window your public-sector team would want to know about — before it firms up"). Keep them. *(Note: this differs from the Cloverleaf marketing-copy rule that bans em dashes. That rule is for external brand copy. Stephen's personal email voice uses them — don't strip them out.)*
- Contractions always ("you're," "it's," "don't," "can't"). They keep it human.
- Company name is always **Cloverleaf AI**.
- Numerals for stats ("$1.6M," "$20,000/year," "15 minutes," "three of their ten accounts").

**What to avoid.**
- Generic openers: "I hope this email finds you well," "Just checking in," "Circling back." (He explicitly mocks "just 'checking in.'")
- SaaS buzzwords: leverage, streamline, synergize, unlock, best-in-class, game-changing.
- Multiple asks. Exclamation-point-heavy hype. Fake urgency.
- Over-explaining B2G terms (RFP, SLED, pilot, procurement) — the audience knows them.

## 3. Signature moves & phrases (pulled from real emails)

Reuse these — they're authentically his:

- **Naming the disqualifier up front:** "One thing that might make the 30 minutes worth it *even if you're not the buyer*..."
- **The honest frame:** "figured I'd try one more time with something concrete instead of just 'checking in.'"
- **The graceful out:** "No hard feelings either way, just don't want to keep cluttering your inbox if it's a no for now."
- **The take-the-hint breakup:** "I've reached out a few times without a response, so I'll take the hint and make this the last one."
- **The re-open door:** "If that changes, one reply gets you a look at what Cloverleaf AI would surface across your territory."
- **Low-pressure demo ask:** "No pitch, just want to show you how it all works and let you decide if it's worth flagging internally."
- **The concrete signal hook:** "[Jurisdiction]'s [official] flagged $[X] in the FY[XX] budget for [thing] this [month]... Budget's not approved yet, no vendor named — the kind of window your public-sector team would want to know about before it firms up."
- **Human close:** "Until then, go catch something worth bragging about in New Mexico." / "Enjoy the weekend."
- **The pre-RFP value line:** "No vendor picked, no RFP posted — just a named decision-maker thinking out loud in a public budget workshop. That's the exact pre-RFP window where [competitor's product] can get in ahead of [incumbents]."

## 4. Scenario playbooks

### A. Cold outreach — peer / shared-background angle

**When:** First or early touch to someone where there's a real connection (ex-colleague, shared industry, mutual network) or a specific reason they'd care.

**Structure:** Warm, specific hook (the connection or the reason) → one sentence on why it's relevant to *them* → soft time ask.

**Real example** (Stephen → Nat, CrowdStrike, 7/23):
> Nat,
>
> Still happy to do the 15-minute walkthrough of Cloverleaf AI whenever works. No pitch, just want to show you how it all works and let you decide if it's worth flagging internally.
>
> I know you've got the relationships in your patch well established but this could be highly impactful for the west team you mentioned being more greenfield!
>
> Do you have time in the next week or two?
>
> Best,
> Stephen

**Real example** (Stephen → Jordan, Trace3, "Former FTNT"):
> Hey Jordan,
>
> Tried giving you a call just now (VM box is full). It looks like you and I share many similar connections (Ex-Fortinet, grew up in the DMV area). I joined Cloverleaf AI, based in Denver...

**Template:**
> [First],
>
> [Specific connection or reason — shared background, something they said, a referral.] No pitch — [what you actually want, framed as low-stakes].
>
> [One sentence on why it could matter to their specific situation.]
>
> Do you have time in the next week or two?
>
> Best,
> Stephen

### H. Pricing / proposal / agreement

**When:** Sending numbers and a path to signature. This is the one place he goes longer — but it's still clean, scannable, and low-pressure.

**Structure:** Warm open → pricing in tight bullets with plain-English notes → explicitly frame numbers as flexible/not-yet-binding → the 1–2 things you need from them → the next step (a scheduled sync) → warm close.

**Real example** (Stephen → Joseph & Brian, ICBA pricing):
> Hi Joseph and Brian,
>
> Really enjoyed the conversation yesterday. Brian, thanks again for the intro.
>
> As promised, the agreement to socialize with your members and board is attached. Pricing:
>
> - $20,000/year platform fee — includes nationwide coverage, so tracking a community in Kentucky or watching something in Iowa trickle east is built in, not an add-on
> - $1,900 per user/year
> - Quoted at 5 users = $29,500/year total
>
> Treat the user count as a starting point, not a locked number. You mentioned possibly beginning at two or three and scaling as the success stories come in — I'll reissue at whatever count the board lands on before anything is signed. Nothing is binding until signature, so this is the figure to put in front of the group and adjust.
>
> Two things I'd need from you, both highlighted on the agreement:
>
> - Your legal entity name
> - A billing/financial contact (name, email, phone)
>
> Once the board settles on a final license count and you're ready, fill those in and I'll send it through DocuSign for signature.
>
> I'll also send a calendar invite for a sync the second week of August to review feedback, confirm the license count, and map the path to approval ahead of September. If you need more runway coming out of the legislative conference, just say the word and I'll move it.
>
> Appreciate you both driving this. Looking forward to turning it into some real wins for the membership.
>
> Best,
> Stephen

**Key tactics to keep:** the "starting point, not a locked number" reassurance, "nothing is binding until signature," anchoring the next step as a real scheduled sync, and closing on *their* outcome ("real wins for the membership").

### I. Negotiation & objection handling / pushback

**When:** They've stalled, gone cold after buy-in, or pushed back on price/timing. Stay calm, stay curious, ask the direct question, keep a door open. Never defensive, never desperate.

**Stephen's move — turn a "no" into a number** (Stephen → Jon, Bremik, after a budget objection):
> Jon, understood. I'll cancel Friday. Was there a target number the company was aiming for that would justify rolling this out across all your markets? Happy to go to Jeremy and see what I can do.

*(Context: Jon had written, "the financial commitment is more than we are comfortable making. We are going to revisit after the first of the year." Stephen accepts gracefully, then probes for the real number and signals he can flex with Jeremy — keeping it alive without pressure.)*

**Jeremy's move — name the reversal honestly and ask why** (Jeremy → Brian, Comcast, after a stall):
> Hey Brian,
>
> Honestly, this one caught me off guard. You seemed bought into the pilot on our June 18th call, and the fit seemed clear on both sides. Can you help me understand what changed?

**Jeremy's move — reframe around opportunity cost** (Jeremy → Ido, persistent but thoughtful):
> Ido,
>
> Checking in here. I know running a startup is ultimately a battle against time. Not just can we move fast enough. Can we spend our time focusing on the right things right now? Can we live with the opportunity costs of the projects we aren't pursuing? If acquiring those next 5 customers is a high enough priority, I can guarantee that your time and capital investment in Cloverleaf AI will return.
>
> What do you think? Worth jumping on a call with your tech leadership?

**Principles:**
- Accept the answer gracefully first ("understood," "no hard feelings"), *then* probe.
- Ask one direct, non-defensive question: "What changed?" / "Was there a target number?"
- Offer to go to bat for them ("Happy to go to Jeremy and see what I can do").
- Reframe around their priorities and cost of inaction, not your quota.
- Never argue with the objection. Get curious about it.

## 6. Quick do / don't cheat sheet

**Do**
- Open with something specific to *them* — a quote, a number, a shared detail.
- Keep it short. One ask. One clear next step.
- Name the disqualifier and give the out ("even if you're not the buyer," "no hard feelings").
- Use real signals: named jurisdiction + named official + dollar figure + "no vendor named yet."
- Stay warm and a little human. End with a genuine line when it fits.
- Accept "no" gracefully, then ask one honest question.

**Don't**
- Open with "I hope this finds you well," "just checking in," or "circling back."
- Stack multiple asks or push hard for the close.
- Use SaaS buzzwords or over-explain B2G terms.
- Sound like a template. Vary the hook to the person.
- Get defensive about an objection — get curious about it.
