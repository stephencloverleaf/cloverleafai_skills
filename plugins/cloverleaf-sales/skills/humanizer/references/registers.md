# Register playbook

Read the matching section before drafting. Register decides which rules bend: a fragment is voice in a cold email and a tell in an essay; a bulleted spec is correct in docs and a cop-out in an argument. When a brand-voice skill covers the same register, its rules win on voice and terminology; these rules fill the gaps.

## Cold outreach email

The reader deletes on reflex. The email earns its next sentence or dies.

- 50-120 words. One idea. One ask.
- Open with the one specific thing the reader didn't expect you to know: something they said, did, published, bought, or budgeted. If you don't have that specific, the problem is research, not phrasing; no style fix rescues an empty email.
- Write like a peer with information, not a vendor with enthusiasm. No compliments about their company, no "I noticed you're doing amazing things."
- Banned: "I hope this finds you well", "quick question", "just following up", "I know you're busy", "touch base", "circle back", "synergies", "solutions", any sentence about your own excitement.
- Subject line: a specific noun from the body, 2-6 words, no clickbait, no colon-subtitle.
- Zero em dashes, zero rhetorical questions, zero exclamation marks. One question total: the ask.
- The ask is small and concrete ("worth 15 minutes Thursday?"), not "explore potential synergies."
- Fragments are allowed here; they read as a person typing fast, not a model performing.

## LinkedIn post

- No broetry: one-sentence-per-line formatting with dramatic white space is the most recognizable AI-adjacent format on the platform.
- The hook is a claim or a specific, not a tease ("What I learned might surprise you") and not a question you immediately answer (§35).
- No engagement bait: "Agree?", "Thoughts?", "Who else has seen this?"
- At most one emoji, and only if the author's own feed uses them.
- 100-250 words. If it needs more, it's an article.
- End on the point, not a moral. "The lesson here is..." closers are §41 in a windbreaker.

## Blog post, essay, newsletter

Rhythm rules (SKILL.md mechanism 5) matter most here; long-form exposes uniform cadence.

- Open with a scene, a specific, or a claim. Never a definition, a dictionary quote, a rhetorical question, or "In today's...".
- Headers optional below ~800 words; when used, plain claims in sentence case, no colon-subtitles (§36).
- Voice belongs here: stance, asides, mixed feelings, humor if the author has it. This is where the PERSONALITY guidance applies fully; bloodless neutrality is its own tell.
- Spend length unevenly. Four paragraphs on the interesting part, one sentence on the obvious part.
- End on the last concrete point or an implication. No recap (§41), no upbeat send-off (§25).

## Technical documentation

Precision beats personality. Neutral and plain is the correct human voice here; don't inject opinions or first person.

- Structure, bullets, and tables are legitimate load-bearing tools, not tells. §37 applies to argument, not reference material.
- Terse subjectless fragments ("No configuration required.") are house style in docs; keep them (§13's rewrite goes the other direction here).
- Bans that still hold: marketing adjectives (robust, seamless, powerful, blazingly fast), §7 vocabulary, signposting (§28), diff-anchored narration (§30), emojis.
- Examples must be runnable and real. A generic example is worse than none.
- Say what the thing does, not what it "allows you to" do, when the shorter form is accurate.

## Report, memo, brief

- Bottom line up front: the finding or recommendation is the first sentence, not the destination.
- Numbers over adjectives. "Pipeline grew 34%" not "significant pipeline growth."
- Recommendations stated as recommendations ("Do X by Friday"), not as considerations (§34).
- Hedge only where uncertainty is the finding, and quantify it if possible ("60-70% likely" beats "may potentially").
- Section headers fine; keep them plain. One-line executive summary beats a recap closer.

## Internal chat (Slack, Teams)

- Fragments, lowercase, and abbreviations are normal. Full corporate sentences read as either a bot or an announcement.
- Skip greetings and sign-offs in an ongoing thread; brevity is respect.
- Lead with the ask or the answer; context after, and only what's needed.
- Emojis follow the channel's culture, not a style rule.
